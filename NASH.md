# John Nash and Nash Equilibrium

## John Nash
- American mathematician (1928–2015) whose early 1950s work reshaped game theory.
- Contributed to PDEs and differential geometry; earned the 1994 Nobel Prize in Economics for game theory and the 2015 Abel Prize (with Nirenberg) for PDE breakthroughs.
- Wrote “Non-Cooperative Games” (1950), providing existence proofs for equilibrium in finite games and defining the concept that bears his name.
- Demonstrated that even without binding agreements, predictable outcomes exist when each player best-responds to others.

## Nash Equilibrium
- A strategy profile where no player can gain by unilateral deviation given others’ strategies.
- Pure equilibria may not exist; Nash proved mixed-strategy equilibria always exist in finite games using fixed-point theory (Brouwer/Kakutani).
- Interpretation: self-consistent beliefs and actions; stability under one-shot deviations.
- In extensive-form games, subgame perfect equilibrium refines Nash by requiring credibility at every subgame.

## Significance and Limits
- Provides a baseline prediction for strategic settings from pricing to auctions and bargaining.
- May be multiple or inefficient (e.g., Prisoner’s Dilemma); equilibrium selection and dynamics are non-trivial.
- Computation can be hard: two-player zero-sum solves via linear programming; general two-player bimatrix uses Lemke–Howson; >2 players or general games can be PPAD-hard.
- Robustness questions include learning convergence, payoff perturbations, and bounded rationality; correlated and coarse correlated equilibria relax the independence assumption.

## What I find most interesting
- The equilibrium existence proof is purely mathematical yet explains real-world regularities in markets and competition.
- Nash bridged abstract fixed-point theory with concrete strategic behavior, inspiring later links between computation, learning, and economics.
- His career arc—brilliant early work, decades battling mental illness, then a return to research—highlights resilience and the lasting impact of foundational ideas.
